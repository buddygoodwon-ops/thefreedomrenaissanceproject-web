The White Paper

Paid off is not the same as ownedThe property tax never ends

Income taxes are set by Congress and the President; ordinary citizens have almost no leverage there. Property taxes are different. They are local, they are visible, and in many states they are set by a handful of county officials.

They also fall hardest on people with fixed incomes. Social Security does not rise with the value of a home, but the tax bill follows that value, and it continues every year for life, even after the mortgage is paid off.

Four problems with the property tax

1. It's regressive

Because a home is a much bigger multiple of income for a working family than for a wealthy one, the property tax takes a larger share of income from people with less. Even researchers who generally favor higher taxes on the wealthy agree on this point.

2. It taxes wealth you haven't realized — and can't easily sell

Mention a tax on unrealized capital gains to an investor and they object instantly: the gain is only on paper, it could disappear, and you'd have to sell to pay. A market-value property tax is worse on every count. It taxes the whole value, not just the gain, every single year. And you can't sell one percent of your house to pay the bill.

3. Its enforcement is draconian

Fall behind on income taxes and the IRS has ten years to collect, can accept less than the full amount through an offer in compromise, offers payment plans, and generally needs a court order to take your primary home. Property tax enforcement offers none of that. There is no settlement for less than what is owed and no forgiveness, and in most states the debt never expires. The remedy is selling the house. Until the Supreme Court's 2023 Tyler v. Hennepin County ruling, some counties even kept the owner's equity above the debt.

California's rules are typical. Taxes unpaid on June 30 put the home in default, and a penalty of 1.5 percent a month (18 percent a year) begins to accrue. After five years in default, the county tax collector gains the power to sell the home at public auction. The only ways to stop it are to pay everything owed or to enter a single installment plan that also requires paying each new year's bill on time. Miss a payment and the plan is cancelled, and the house can go to auction.

4. It changes what ownership means

If you must pay every year to keep something you already bought — with income that was already taxed — and the government can sell it if you stop, then in practice you hold it subject to rent owed to the state.

California shows a better principle

In California, Proposition 13 (1978) capped the general levy at 1 percent of a home's assessed value at purchase, plus voter-approved debt, with assessment growth limited to 2 percent a year. It kept longtime owners from being taxed out of homes that had tripled in value.

New communities pay for their schools, fire stations and water systems through Mello-Roos districts: bonds repaid by a special tax that, for facilities, ends when the bonds are paid off. My own Mello-Roos runs about 0.6 percent of my home's value. The general levy on top of it is roughly twice that — and it never ends.

Once the buildings are paid for, what exactly is the perpetual levy buying — and who decided it should never stop?

Read the real books

Every state and most cities and counties publish an audited Annual Comprehensive Financial Report (ACFR). Where the budget tells you what was spent, the ACFR shows what the government owns — land, buildings, investments, reserves — and what it owes. It's what bond buyers read before lending. Taxpayers should read it too, before anyone tells them a tax increase is unavoidable.
