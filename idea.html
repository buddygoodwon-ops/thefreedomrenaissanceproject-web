Paid off is not the same as ownedThe property tax never ends

Why it's unjust and un-American

To own something is to hold it free and clear. A home bought with income that was already taxed, and paid off in full, should meet that test. It does not. Every year the owner must pay again to keep it, and if the payments stop, the government sells it. That is not ownership. It is rent owed to the state on property you already bought.

No other major asset is treated this way. Stocks, bonds, savings, retirement accounts, gold and art are not taxed simply for being owned; they are taxed when they are sold or when they produce income. The family home, the asset most Americans spend their working lives paying for, is taxed every year for as long as they hold it.

The enforcement is draconian. Fall behind on income taxes and the IRS has ten years to collect, can accept less than the full amount through an offer in compromise, offers payment plans, and generally needs a court order to take a primary home. Fall behind on property taxes and there is no settlement for less than what is owed, no forgiveness, and in most states the debt never expires. Penalties pile up, and after a few years of nonpayment the county can sell the home at public auction, in many states without any court judgment. The terms are simple: pay, or lose your house. Until the Supreme Court's 2023 Tyler v. Hennepin County ruling, some counties even kept the owner's equity above the debt.

It falls hardest on people with fixed incomes. Social Security does not rise with the value of a home, but the tax bill follows that value, and it continues every year for life, even after the mortgage is paid off.

It also fails as a tax

1. It's regressive

Because a home is a much bigger multiple of income for a working family than for a wealthy one, the property tax takes a larger share of income from people with less. Even researchers who generally favor higher taxes on the wealthy agree on this point.

2. It taxes wealth you haven't realized — and can't easily sell

Mention a tax on unrealized capital gains to an investor and they object instantly: the gain is only on paper, it could disappear, and you'd have to sell to pay. A market-value property tax is worse on every count. It taxes the whole value, not just the gain, every single year. And you can't sell one percent of your house to pay the bill.

If you have to keep paying to keep it, do you own it — or are you renting it from the government?

Read the real books

Every state and most cities and counties publish an audited Annual Comprehensive Financial Report (ACFR). Where the budget tells you what was spent, the ACFR shows what the government owns — land, buildings, investments, reserves — and what it owes. It's what bond buyers read before lending. Taxpayers should read it too, before anyone tells them a tax increase is unavoidable.
